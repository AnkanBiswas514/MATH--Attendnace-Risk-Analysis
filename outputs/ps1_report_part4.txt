REFLECTION Q1 — Which population can these records represent, and what
cannot be generalised?
  Reasonably represent: a small, homogeneous undergraduate cohort (three
  sections, morning study session) in which attendance, study hours, sleep
  and exam scores co-vary as they do here. The findings generalise to similar
  college populations with the same delivery mode and assessment structure.
  Cannot generalise to: evening/part-time students, large lecture cohorts,
  different disciplines, online-only delivery, or any population where the
  attendance–performance relationship differs. N=24 is too small for precise
  estimates, and the data are synthetic, so effect sizes should not be treated
  as population facts.

REFLECTION Q2 — Which sampling design best fits this question, and what
bias could remain?
  Best design: stratified random sampling by Section (the grouping variable),
  because sections differ in mean exam score and attendance; stratification
  guarantees each section is represented in proportion to its size and
  reduces sampling variability of the attendance–passing association.
  Remaining bias: (1) small-sample bias (n=24) — estimates are noisy;
  (2) all students are Morning session → session bias;
  (3) synthetic data → may not reflect real-world behaviour;
  (4) attendance measured as a single percentage → misclassification risk;
  (5) non-response/self-selection not modelled.

REFLECTION Q4 — What assumptions make the binomial calculation valid, and how realistic are they?
  (1) Fixed n (24 students) — satisfied.
  (2) Constant pass probability pi for every student — unrealistic: pass
      probability clearly depends on attendance/study hours.
  (3) Independent outcomes — plausible if students work independently, but
      shared section/instructor effects could induce dependence.
  (4) Binary outcome (pass/fail) — satisfied.
  Verdict: the binomial is a reasonable first approximation but the constant-
  pi assumption is violated because attendance predicts passing.

REFLECTION Q5 — Does correlation establish that one variable causes another? Why not?
  No. Correlation measures linear association only. A third variable (e.g.,
  motivation, prior ability, section/instructor) could drive both attendance
  and exam score (confounding). With n=24 and an observational (not
  randomised) design, causality cannot be inferred. A randomised experiment
  assigning attendance support would be needed to claim causation.

REFLECTION Q6 — How do sample size and the CLT change the sampling distribution?
  As n increases: (1) the sampling distribution of the mean becomes narrower
  (SE = sigma/sqrt n shrinks); (2) its shape approaches normality regardless
  of the population shape (CLT); (3) simulated SE converges to theoretical
  SE. With n=24 here, the normal approximation is reasonable but not exact;
  small n gives wide intervals and low power.

REFLECTION Q7 — Why do the normal MLE variance and unbiased sample variance differ?
  MLE variance divides by n; unbiased sample variance divides by n-1.
  Dividing by n under-estimates sigma^2 because the sample mean (used in
  the formula) is itself estimated from the same data and sits closer to
  the observations than the true mean would. The n-1 correction (Bessel's
  correction) removes this downward bias. Here ratio = (n-1)/n = 23/24 ≈
  0.9583, so MLE variance is ~4.2% smaller.

REFLECTION Q8 — Which estimator would you report, considering bias, variance and MSE?
  Report the unbiased sample variance (divides by n-1) together with the
  sample mean. Rationale: it is unbiased for sigma^2; its variance is only
  marginally larger than the MLE's; and its MSE (bias^2 + variance) is
  competitive because the MLE's bias, though small, is non-zero. For the
  mean itself, the sample mean is unbiased, consistent, and has the lowest
  variance among unbiased estimators (Gauss-Markov / MLE), so report the
  sample mean with its bootstrap SE.

REFLECTION Q9 — How do confidence level, sample size and variability change interval width?
  Width = 2 * critical * SE. Higher confidence -> larger critical value -> wider interval
  (90% < 95% < 99% shown above). Larger n -> smaller SE -> narrower interval. Larger
  variability (SD) -> larger SE -> wider interval. Trade-off: confidence vs precision.

REFLECTION Q10 — What do Type I and Type II errors mean for this specific claim?
  Claim: mean exam score >= 60.
  Type I error (false positive): rejecting H0 when mu really is 60 — concluding
  the cohort passes on average when it does not (alpha = 0.05 risk).
  Type II error (false negative): failing to reject H0 when mu > 60 — missing
  a genuinely passing cohort. With n=24, power is limited, so Type II risk is
  meaningful for effects near the boundary.

REFLECTION Q11 — Do the confidence interval and hypothesis-test decision agree? Explain.
  Yes. The 95% t-CI [57.21, 69.04] lies entirely above 60,
  and the one-tailed t-test rejects H0 (p < 0.05). A two-sided 95% CI excluding
  the null value 60 is consistent with rejecting H0 at alpha=0.05 in the matching
  direction; both lead to the same conclusion: mean exam score > 60.
