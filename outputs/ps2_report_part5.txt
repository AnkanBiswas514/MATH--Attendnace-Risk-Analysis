REFLECTION Q1 — Are the groups mutually exclusive and appropriately defined?
  Yes: High (>=median) and Low (<median) attendance are mutually exclusive and
  exhaustive (every student is in exactly one group). Using the median as the
  threshold gives balanced group sizes (14 high, 10 low here), which is good
  for power. A fixed benchmark (e.g., 75%) might be more interpretable but
  could yield unbalanced groups.

REFLECTION Q2 — Which sampling strategy reduces the risk of under-representing a group?
  Stratified random sampling by the grouping variable (attendance group).
  It forces proportional (or equal) inclusion of both High and Low groups,
  unlike SRS which could by chance miss a group, and unlike cluster sampling
  which could select only one section.

REFLECTION Q6 — Why might estimates from small groups fluctuate more than estimates from the full sample?
  SE = sigma/sqrt(n): smaller n -> larger SE -> wider sampling distribution ->
  more fluctuation. The low-attendance group (n=10) will have noisier mean/
  pass-rate estimates than the full sample (n=24). Small-group estimates are
  less reliable; confidence intervals will be wider.

REFLECTION Q7 — What does the CLT tell you, and what does it not guarantee for this dataset?
  The CLT says the sampling distribution of the mean approaches normality as
  n grows, regardless of population shape, and SE shrinks as sigma/sqrt(n).
  It does NOT guarantee normality at n=24 (small n), nor does it fix bias,
  outliers, or dependence. With n=24 the approximation is tentative.

REFLECTION Q8 — How does the bias-variance trade-off affect the estimator you select?
  The sample mean is unbiased and efficient (lowest variance among unbiased
  estimators under normal theory) — report it. For variance, the unbiased
  sample variance (÷n-1) trades a tiny variance increase to remove the MLE's
  downward bias; its MSE is competitive. Prefer unbiased estimators here
  because n is small and bias is more damaging than a small variance rise.

REFLECTION Q9 — How does interval overlap relate to, but not replace, a formal test?
  The two 95% CIs do not overlap.
  Non-overlapping 95% CIs usually imply a significant difference, but overlapping
  CIs do NOT imply non-significance (the correct SE for the difference is smaller
  than the sum of individual SEs). Always run the formal t-test — here: significant.

REFLECTION Q10 — What practical meaning does the test decision have for the groups?
  If significant: attendance group is associated with exam performance — supports
  attendance-focused interventions. If not significant: no evidence of a mean
  difference at alpha=0.05, but with n=24 a real difference could be missed
  (low power). Practical relevance also depends on effect size (Cohen's d).

REFLECTION Q11 — Describe a Type I and Type II error in this comparison.
  Type I error: concluding attendance groups differ in exam score when they
  really do not (false alarm; alpha = 0.05).
  Type II error: concluding no difference when high-attendance students truly
  perform better (missed detection). With small n, Type II risk is high.
  For this claim, a Type II error could mean overlooking a valuable attendance
  intervention; a Type I error could mean investing in one that doesn't work.

REFLECTION Q12 — Why is ANOVA preferable to many pairwise t-tests here?
  Running multiple pairwise t-tests inflates the family-wise Type I error
  rate (chance of at least one false positive). ANOVA tests the omnibus
  null (all group means equal) in one test, controlling alpha. With only two
  groups here, ANOVA and the two-sample t-test are equivalent, but ANOVA is
  the correct framework if more groups are added.

5.2 Chi-square tests for categorical variables

  Passed vs Section: chi2=2.021, p=0.3640, min expected=1.67
  Expected-count condition: if any expected count < 5, treat the p-value
  cautiously or use Fisher's exact test instead.

REFLECTION Q13 — Are chi-square expected counts adequate? If not, what caution?
  With n=24 split across groups/sections, some expected counts fall below 5,
  violating the chi-square approximation. Caution: p-values may be inaccurate;
  use Fisher's exact test or report exact methods, and interpret cautiously.

5.3 Power discussion
With alpha=0.05, per-group n=10 and Cohen's d≈2.93, power increases with larger sample size, larger effect size, lower variability, and larger alpha. Small groups (as in this dataset) yield low power for small effects, so a non-significant result should be interpreted cautiously.
  Observed Cohen's d for attendance groups = 2.93.
  Power rises with larger n, larger effect size, lower variability, and
  larger alpha. With n=10/14, power to detect a small effect is low;
  the study is powered only for medium-to-large effects.

REFLECTION Q14 — Would the study have enough power to detect a small group difference?
  No. With ~10-14 students per group, power to detect a small effect (d=0.2)
  at alpha=0.05 is very low (well under 0.8). A non-significant result would
  be inconclusive, not evidence of no difference. A larger sample is needed.

REFLECTION Q15 — Statistical significance vs practical importance here?
  Statistical significance (p<0.05) tells us an observed difference is unlikely
  under H0. Practical importance depends on effect size: Cohen's d = 2.93 and the raw attendance-group exam gap. Even a significant tiny
  gap may not justify an intervention; a large gap matters even if p is marginal
  given small n.

REFLECTION Q16 — What larger/better-designed study would improve power and generalisability?
  Larger multi-section/multi-institution sample (n in hundreds); randomised
  attendance encouragement for causality; record covariates (prior GPA, motivation);
  pre-register the analysis; oversample under-represented groups; use the
  power analysis above to set target n for 80% power at the smallest effect of
  practical interest.

OVERALL CONCLUSION — PS04 Attendance Threshold Investigation
========================================================================

  Using the median attendance threshold (79.0%), the High-attendance group
  (n=14) had mean exam score 72.8 vs 49.6 for Low (n=10),
  and pass rates 100% vs 50%. The two-sample t-test rejected the null of equal means (p=0.000, Cohen's d = 2.93), and chi-square rejected equal pass rates. Together these indicate that students above the attendance
  threshold perform better academically. Limitations: n=24, synthetic data,
  observational design (causality not established), low power for small effects.
  A larger randomised study is recommended before acting on the finding.
