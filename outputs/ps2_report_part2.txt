REFLECTION Q1 — Are the groups mutually exclusive and appropriately defined?
  Yes: High (>=median) and Low (<median) attendance are mutually exclusive and
  exhaustive (every student is in exactly one group). Using the median as the
  threshold gives balanced group sizes (14 high, 10 low here), which is good
  for power. A fixed benchmark (e.g., 75%) might be more interpretable but
  could yield unbalanced groups.

REFLECTION Q2 — Which sampling strategy reduces the risk of under-representing a group?
  Stratified random sampling by the grouping variable (attendance group).
  It forces proportional (or equal) inclusion of both High and Low groups,
  unlike SRS which could by chance miss a group, and unlike cluster sampling
  which could select only one section.

REFLECTION Q3 — Are group distributions similar enough for the planned parametric comparison?
  Both groups look roughly unimodal; Shapiro-Wilk p-values above do not reject
  normality within groups. Variances: check homogeneity before pooling.
  With n=10 and n=14, parametric tests are a reasonable working approximation
  but results should be confirmed with non-parametric checks given the small n.

REFLECTION Q4 — What independence and constant-probability assumptions are needed for the binomial model?
  (1) Fixed n — satisfied.
  (2) Constant pass probability pi for every student — violated: attendance
  group strongly predicts passing.
  (3) Independent outcomes — plausible but shared section effects could induce
  dependence. The binomial is therefore a rough approximation only.

REFLECTION Q5 — How should an unusually high or low observation affect interpretation?
  An extreme outlier can pull the mean and inflate the SD, distorting t-tests
  and confidence intervals. With n=24, one outlier has substantial leverage.
  Always report median alongside mean, and run sensitivity checks with/without
  flagged outliers (e.g., the low scorer STU001).
