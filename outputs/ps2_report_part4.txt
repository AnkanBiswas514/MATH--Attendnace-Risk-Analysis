REFLECTION Q1 — Are the groups mutually exclusive and appropriately defined?
  Yes: High (>=median) and Low (<median) attendance are mutually exclusive and
  exhaustive (every student is in exactly one group). Using the median as the
  threshold gives balanced group sizes (14 high, 10 low here), which is good
  for power. A fixed benchmark (e.g., 75%) might be more interpretable but
  could yield unbalanced groups.

REFLECTION Q2 — Which sampling strategy reduces the risk of under-representing a group?
  Stratified random sampling by the grouping variable (attendance group).
  It forces proportional (or equal) inclusion of both High and Low groups,
  unlike SRS which could by chance miss a group, and unlike cluster sampling
  which could select only one section.

REFLECTION Q4 — What independence and constant-probability assumptions are needed for the binomial model?
  (1) Fixed n — satisfied.
  (2) Constant pass probability pi for every student — violated: attendance
  group strongly predicts passing.
  (3) Independent outcomes — plausible but shared section effects could induce
  dependence. The binomial is therefore a rough approximation only.

REFLECTION Q5 — How should an unusually high or low observation affect interpretation?
  An extreme outlier can pull the mean and inflate the SD, distorting t-tests
  and confidence intervals. With n=24, one outlier has substantial leverage.
  Always report median alongside mean, and run sensitivity checks with/without
  flagged outliers (e.g., the low scorer STU001).

REFLECTION Q6 — Why might estimates from small groups fluctuate more than estimates from the full sample?
  SE = sigma/sqrt(n): smaller n -> larger SE -> wider sampling distribution ->
  more fluctuation. The low-attendance group (n=10) will have noisier mean/
  pass-rate estimates than the full sample (n=24). Small-group estimates are
  less reliable; confidence intervals will be wider.

REFLECTION Q7 — What does the CLT tell you, and what does it not guarantee for this dataset?
  The CLT says the sampling distribution of the mean approaches normality as
  n grows, regardless of population shape, and SE shrinks as sigma/sqrt(n).
  It does NOT guarantee normality at n=24 (small n), nor does it fix bias,
  outliers, or dependence. With n=24 the approximation is tentative.

REFLECTION Q8 — How does the bias-variance trade-off affect the estimator you select?
  The sample mean is unbiased and efficient (lowest variance among unbiased
  estimators under normal theory) — report it. For variance, the unbiased
  sample variance (÷n-1) trades a tiny variance increase to remove the MLE's
  downward bias; its MSE is competitive. Prefer unbiased estimators here
  because n is small and bias is more damaging than a small variance rise.

REFLECTION Q9 — How does interval overlap relate to, but not replace, a formal test?
  The two 95% CIs do not overlap.
  Non-overlapping 95% CIs usually imply a significant difference, but overlapping
  CIs do NOT imply non-significance (the correct SE for the difference is smaller
  than the sum of individual SEs). Always run the formal t-test — here: significant.

REFLECTION Q10 — What practical meaning does the test decision have for the groups?
  If significant: attendance group is associated with exam performance — supports
  attendance-focused interventions. If not significant: no evidence of a mean
  difference at alpha=0.05, but with n=24 a real difference could be missed
  (low power). Practical relevance also depends on effect size (Cohen's d).

REFLECTION Q11 — Describe a Type I and Type II error in this comparison.
  Type I error: concluding attendance groups differ in exam score when they
  really do not (false alarm; alpha = 0.05).
  Type II error: concluding no difference when high-attendance students truly
  perform better (missed detection). With small n, Type II risk is high.
  For this claim, a Type II error could mean overlooking a valuable attendance
  intervention; a Type I error could mean investing in one that doesn't work.
