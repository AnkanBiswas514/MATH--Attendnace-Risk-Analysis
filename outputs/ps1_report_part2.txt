REFLECTION Q1 — Which population can these records represent, and what
cannot be generalised?
  Reasonably represent: a small, homogeneous undergraduate cohort (three
  sections, morning study session) in which attendance, study hours, sleep
  and exam scores co-vary as they do here. The findings generalise to similar
  college populations with the same delivery mode and assessment structure.
  Cannot generalise to: evening/part-time students, large lecture cohorts,
  different disciplines, online-only delivery, or any population where the
  attendance–performance relationship differs. N=24 is too small for precise
  estimates, and the data are synthetic, so effect sizes should not be treated
  as population facts.

REFLECTION Q2 — Which sampling design best fits this question, and what
bias could remain?
  Best design: stratified random sampling by Section (the grouping variable),
  because sections differ in mean exam score and attendance; stratification
  guarantees each section is represented in proportion to its size and
  reduces sampling variability of the attendance–passing association.
  Remaining bias: (1) small-sample bias (n=24) — estimates are noisy;
  (2) all students are Morning session → session bias;
  (3) synthetic data → may not reflect real-world behaviour;
  (4) attendance measured as a single percentage → misclassification risk;
  (5) non-response/self-selection not modelled.

REFLECTION Q3 — What graphical evidence supports/challenges the distributional assumptions?
  Histogram/boxplot: no severe skew; one low outlier (STU001, score 42) and
  one high (STU016, score 87) sit within/near the IQR fence.
  Q-Q plot: points close to the 45-degree line support approximate normality.

  model is defensible as a working approximation but not as a fact.

REFLECTION Q4 — What assumptions make the binomial calculation valid, and how realistic are they?
  (1) Fixed n (24 students) — satisfied.
  (2) Constant pass probability pi for every student — unrealistic: pass
      probability clearly depends on attendance/study hours.
  (3) Independent outcomes — plausible if students work independently, but
      shared section/instructor effects could induce dependence.
  (4) Binary outcome (pass/fail) — satisfied.
  Verdict: the binomial is a reasonable first approximation but the constant-
  pi assumption is violated because attendance predicts passing.

REFLECTION Q5 — Does correlation establish that one variable causes another? Why not?
  No. Correlation measures linear association only. A third variable (e.g.,
  motivation, prior ability, section/instructor) could drive both attendance
  and exam score (confounding). With n=24 and an observational (not
  randomised) design, causality cannot be inferred. A randomised experiment
  assigning attendance support would be needed to claim causation.
