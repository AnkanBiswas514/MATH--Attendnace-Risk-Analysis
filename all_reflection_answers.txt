PS03 – Attendance Risk Analysis: Reflection Answers

3. Repeated samples and estimators
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How do sample size and the CLT change the sampling distribution?
Simulating 5,000 samples of the exam mean: empirical SE vs theory = 8.07 vs 7.92
(n = 3), 6.13 vs 6.13 (n = 5),
4.32 vs 4.34 (n = 10), 3.01 vs 3.07 (n = 20), 2.82 vs 2.80 (n = 24). The
distribution stays centred on 63.1 (unbiased) but narrows as 1/sqrt(n) and
becomes more normal (the CLT). Same for the proportion: SE 0.236 (n = 3)
falling to 0.083 (n = 24), matching sqrt(p(1-p)/n). The CLT does not remove
the need for independence and does not make an unrepresentative sample
representative.

Why do the normal MLE variance and unbiased sample variance differ?
The MLE maximises the likelihood and gives sigma^2 = sum((x - xbar)^2)/n =
187.94, which is biased low because deviations are measured from xbar (fitted
to the same data, using up one degree of freedom). Dividing by n-1 gives the
unbiased s^2 = 196.11; the two differ by the factor (n-1)/n = 0.958, a gap
that shrinks as n grows. The MLE of mu (63.125) is the sample mean.

Which estimator would you report, considering bias, variance and MSE?
Normal-model simulation (true sigma^2 = 196.1): n = 5: MLE bias -39.1, MSE
13,877 vs unbiased bias 0.16, MSE 19,294; n = 10: MLE bias -19.4, MSE 7,388
vs unbiased MSE 8,657; n = 24: MLE bias -8.3, MSE 3,135 vs unbiased 3,339.
The MLE trades a little bias for lower variance and lower MSE, so is slightly
more accurate for point estimation, but s^2 is the one used for t-intervals
and tests, so I report s^2 (and the sample mean, with bootstrap bias approx
0.03 and variance 7.86 - about half the variance of the sample median, 16.5).

4. Uncertainty and the central claim
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How do confidence level, sample size and variability change interval width?
Higher confidence widens intervals: exam-mean margins of error are +/- 4.90
(90%), +/- 5.91 (95%) and +/- 8.02 (99%) with the t critical values 1.714,
2.069, 2.807 (df = 23). Larger n and smaller SD narrow it (ME prop s/sqrt n).
The pass-rate 95% intervals are wide: Wald 0.629-0.954 (ME 0.162), Wilson
0.595-0.908, Clopper-Pearson 0.578-0.929; at 99% Clopper-Pearson is 0.515-0.952.
Wilson/exact are preferred to Wald with n = 24 and p near 0.8. t is used
because sigma is unknown; a z-interval (+/- 5.60) is shown only under an
explicitly assumed known sigma = 14.

What do Type I and Type II errors mean for this specific claim?
Central claim: attendance is positively associated with passing. H0: mean
attendance of passers <= failers (equivalently rho = 0); H1: passers attend more
(one-sided Welch, alpha = 0.05). Passers average 82.95% (n = 19) vs failers
67.40% (n = 5): t = 6.151, df = 14.92, critical value 1.754, p = 0.00001 ->
reject H0 (difference 15.6 points, 95% CI 10.16 to 20.94; d = 2.05; Mann-Whitney
p = 0.001). Test of rho = 0: r = 0.656, t = 4.081, df = 22, critical +/- 2.074,
p = 0.0005. Type I error: concluding attendance matters when it does not (e.g.
launching an unneeded attendance campaign). Type II error: missing a real link
and so failing to support at-risk, low-attendance students. Logistic fit: odds
of passing multiply by about 13.6 per +5 attendance points, but the 95% CI
(1.04-177) shows how uncertain this is with only 5 failures.

Do the confidence interval and hypothesis-test decision agree? Explain.
Yes. For the exam-mean benchmark H0: mu = 60 (two-sided), t = 1.093, critical
+/- 2.069, p = 0.286 -> fail to reject, and 60 lies inside the 95% CI
(57.21, 69.04). For the attendance difference, the CI for the difference
(10.16, 20.94) excludes 0, matching the rejection. For the pass-rate benchmark
H0: p = 0.70 vs H1: p > 0.70, X = 19, exact p = 0.229 (z = 0.98 < 1.645) ->
fail to reject, and 0.70 is within the interval 0.578-0.929. A two-sided test
at alpha and a (1-alpha) interval always agree; mixing a one-sided test with a
two-sided interval can look inconsistent.

5. Group comparison and conclusion
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Are ANOVA and chi-square assumptions plausible for these data?
ANOVA by Section: exam F(2,21) = 0.199, p = 0.821, eta^2 = 0.019 (Section
means 63.6, 65.1, 60.6; Levene p = 0.977; Kruskal p = 0.903); attendance
F(2,21) = 0.502, p = 0.612. Normality (residuals approximately normal) and
equal variance are plausible, independence is questionable. Chi-square:
Section x Passed has expected counts 1.67 (fail) / 6.33 (pass) per Section -
3 of 6 cells below 5 - so chi^2 = 2.02 (p = 0.364) is not reliable; use exact
tests. Attendance group (>=75 vs <75) x Passed: observed 0/16 vs 5/3, expected
3.33/12.67 and 1.67/6.33; chi^2 = 12.63, p = 0.0004, Fisher exact p = 0.0013,
V = 0.725. Goodness-of-fit to a 70/30 benchmark: chi^2 = 0.96, p = 0.327.

What is the difference between statistical significance and practical importance here?
Statistical significance says an association is unlikely to be chance
(p = 0.0005-0.0013); practical importance concerns size and usefulness. The
effect sizes here are very large (d = 2.05; V = 0.725; every student with
attendance >= 75% passed vs 3 of 8 below it), so the finding is both
significant and potentially important, but with only 5 failures the size is
imprecise (odds-ratio CI 1.04-177) and the data are synthetic, so effect
magnitudes should not be taken at face value. Non-significant results (Section
effect) should not be read as proof of no difference.

What larger or better-designed study would improve power and generalisability?
Power with 19 passers vs 5 failers (alpha = 0.05) is 0.07 for d = 0.2, 0.16
for d = 0.5, 0.33 for d = 0.8, 0.63 for d = 1.2 and 0.97 for d = 2.0; the
pass-rate test (p0 = 0.70 vs true 0.80, n = 24) has power 0.26. About 64
students per group would give 80% power for d = 0.5. A better design: random
sample of several hundred students from multiple courses and institutions,
stratified by Section/programme, with attendance measured over time, prior
achievement recorded as a covariate (or an intervention randomised) and a
pre-specified analysis, which would allow adjusted logistic regression and
causal discussion.

PS04 – Attendance Threshold Investigation: Reflection Answers
================================================================================
Dataset_09 (n = 24 students). Threshold = 75% attendance; groups High (n = 16)
and Low (n = 8). Random seed 42. Numbers are from SRN_PS04.py.

================================================================================
1. Comparison groups and sampling frame
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Are the groups mutually exclusive and appropriately defined?
Yes. Using a 75% threshold, High (Attendance >= 75) has 16 students and Low (<
75) has 8; every student falls in exactly one group (no overlap, none missing).
The cut-off uses the closed-open rule >=75 / <75 so boundary cases are
unambiguous. Caveats: 75% is a convention, not a data-driven value, so results
should be checked at other cut-offs; the split is unbalanced (16 vs 8); and
dichotomising a continuous variable discards information (attendance and exam
score correlate at r = 0.978). Group means: attendance 85.25 vs 68.62; exam
70.88 vs 47.62; pass rate 100% (16/16) vs 37.5% (3/8).

Which sampling strategy would reduce the risk of under-representing a group?
Stratified sampling on the attendance group. With n = 8 from 24 (seed 42), the
stratified sample kept the High/Low mix at 62.5%/37.5% (population 66.7%/33.3%)
and gave mean exam 59.6 (population 63.1), pass rate 0.75 (0.79). The simple
random sample over-represented Low students (75% Low) and gave mean exam 51.8
and pass rate 0.50. Over 2,000 repeats, a simple random sample of 8 contained
no Low student 2.2% of the time, stratified 0%; the SD of the sample-mean error
was 3.97 (SRS), 2.38 (stratified) and 1.87 (cluster by Section). The cluster
sample (one Section) was close here only because the Sections are internally
mixed; with few clusters it is generally the least reliable design.

================================================================================
2. Distribution shape and probabilities
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Are group distributions similar enough for the planned parametric comparison?
Mostly. Attendance in both groups is roughly symmetric (skew 0.26 and -0.30),
Shapiro-Wilk p = 0.398 (High) and 0.721 (Low), no IQR outliers, max |z| =
1.76; Q-Q plots follow the line. The overall distribution looks normal (Shapiro
p = 0.626). However the spread differs: SD 6.66 vs 3.42 (Levene p = 0.030),
which is partly an artefact of cutting a continuous variable. Hence Welch's
t-test (no equal-variance assumption) is used, with Mann-Whitney as a robustness
check. Normal model N(79.71, 9.83^2): P(Attendance >= 75) = 0.684 vs observed
0.667; P(>= 90) = 0.147 vs 0.208. The correlation matrix shows very strong
associations (Attendance-Exam r = 0.978, covariance 134.6; Study-Exam 0.944;
Sleep-Exam 0.778). Mardia's skewness test (p = 0.289) and kurtosis (z =
-1.19) do not reject multivariate normality, but with n = 24 and four variables
this test has low power, so plausibility only.

What independence and constant-probability assumptions are needed for the binomial model?
Each of the n = 24 students must be an independent Bernoulli trial with the same
pass probability p (estimated 19/24 = 0.792). Under Bin(24, 0.792) the expected
number of passes is 19.0 (SD 1.99) and observed is 19, P(X = 19) = 0.197.
Checking by group exposes the problem: with a common p, High would be expected
to have 12.7 passes (observed 16, P(X >= 16) = 0.024) and Low 6.3 (observed 3,
P(X <= 3) = 0.012). So the constant-p assumption fails - pass probability
clearly depends on attendance - and students in the same Section may not be
independent. The binomial fits overall counts only because p was estimated from
the same data.

How should an unusually high or low observation affect interpretation?
No observation was flagged (no IQR outliers, all |z| < 2), so nothing was
removed. In general an extreme value should be checked for data-entry error
first, then reported with and without it; means, SDs and Pearson correlation are
sensitive to it, whereas medians, IQR, Spearman and Mann-Whitney are robust.
With n = 8 in the Low group a single extreme point would move that group mean
noticeably.

3. Sampling variability and estimator quality
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why might estimates from small groups fluctuate more than estimates from the full sample?
Because SE = sigma/sqrt(n) (and sqrt(p(1-p)/n) for a proportion). Simulating
5,000 resamples of n = 8 gave an empirical SE of the mean exam score of 4.79
(theory 4.85) overall, matching theory closely at every n (e.g. overall n = 3:
8.07 vs 7.92; n = 16: 3.47 vs 3.43). Group-specific bootstrap SEs of the mean
were 2.44 for High (n = 16) and 1.53 for Low (n = 8; Low has the smaller SD,
4.6 vs 9.9, so its SE is smaller despite the smaller n). The pass-rate SE for
samples of 3 is 0.236 versus 0.102 at n = 16 - small samples fluctuate much
more, and the Low group's pass rate (3/8) is especially imprecise.

What does the CLT tell you, and what does it not guarantee?
The CLT says the sampling distribution of the mean approaches N(mu, sigma^2/n)
as n grows, even for non-normal data, so the t and z machinery is justified for
means. It does not guarantee normality at n = 8 or 16 for skewed or heavy-tailed
data, does not apply to a binary variable with a pass rate near 0 or 1 at small
n (High group pass rate is 100%, so its SE is 0 - the normal approximation is
useless there), does not repair bias from a non-random or non-independent
sample, and does not make the 24 synthetic records representative of any real
population.

How does the bias-variance trade-off affect the estimator you select?
Point estimates: mean exam = 63.125, pass rate = 0.792. Normal MLE: mu_hat =
63.125, sigma_hat^2 = 187.94 (sigma_hat = 13.71), versus unbiased s^2 =
196.11 (ratio (n-1)/n = 0.958). The MLE variance is biased low (bootstrap bias
approx -8.3 = -sigma^2/n) but has smaller variance (1,344 vs 1,464), giving
smaller MSE (1,413 vs 1,464); the unbiased s^2 has bias approx 0 (simulation
noise only). I report the unbiased s^2 for inference (CIs and t-tests need it,
and the MLE's advantage vanishes as n grows), and use the sample mean
(bootstrap bias approx 0, MSE 7.9) as the location estimator.

4. Intervals and tests
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How does overlap or non-overlap of intervals relate to, but not replace, a formal test?
95% t-intervals: High 70.88 +/- 5.29 (65.58, 76.17); Low 47.62 +/- 3.84
(43.78, 51.47); All 63.12 +/- 5.91. The intervals do not overlap, which is
consistent with a significant difference, but overlap is not the test: the
correct comparison uses the SE of the difference (Welch 95% CI for the
difference: 17.10 to 29.40). Two intervals can overlap slightly and still give
a significant difference, so the formal test is required. Interval widths: 99%
(+/- 7.32 for High) > 95% (5.29) > 90% (4.35); the smaller Low group has a
wider t multiplier (df = 7). A z-interval needs a known population sigma; since
sigma is unknown, t is used (plugging s into z gives ME 4.87 vs t's 5.29 -
too narrow).

What practical meaning does the test decision have for the groups?
H0: mu_High = mu_Low; H1: mu_High != mu_Low (alpha = 0.05, Welch). Difference
= 23.25 points, t = 7.835, df = 21.97, critical value +/- 2.074, p < 0.0001 ->
reject H0 (Mann-Whitney p = 0.0001 agrees; Cohen d = 2.70). Students at or
above 75% attendance scored about 23 points higher on average and all passed,
versus 37.5% passing below 75%. This is an association in observational data,
not proof that raising attendance would raise scores (motivation, ability and
study time are confounded). The benchmark check H0: mu = 60 gave t = 1.093,
p = 0.286 (fail to reject).

Describe a Type I and Type II error in this comparison.
Type I: concluding that high- and low-attendance students differ in exam
performance when in truth they do not (probability alpha = 0.05) - the school
might wrongly build an attendance policy around a spurious difference. Type II:
concluding there is no difference when there really is one - missed students
at risk. With a very large observed effect (d = 2.7) a Type II error is
unlikely here, but for a small true effect it is very likely given 16 vs 8
students (see power below).

================================================================================
5. Group structure and power
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why is ANOVA preferable to repeatedly running many pairwise t-tests?
Each extra t-test adds a chance of a false positive; with k groups there are
k(k-1)/2 tests and the family-wise error rate rises (three tests at 0.05 gives
about 14%). One-way ANOVA tests all means together at a single alpha and uses
pooled variance. Results: exam by attendance band (<70, 70-84, >=85; n = 5,
11, 8): F(2,21) = 52.58, p < 0.001, eta^2 = 0.83 (Levene p = 0.442; residual
Shapiro p = 0.415; Kruskal p < 0.001); by Section: F(2,21) = 0.199, p = 0.821,
eta^2 = 0.02, i.e. no Section effect. Assumptions: independence, approximately
normal residuals, equal variances. A significant ANOVA would be followed by a
corrected post-hoc test (e.g. Tukey).

Are chi-square expected counts adequate? If not, what alternative or caution is needed?
No. For attendance group x Passed the expected counts are High: 3.33 fail /
12.67 pass; Low: 1.67 / 6.33 - two of four cells are below 5 (observed: High
0 fail / 16 pass; Low 5 / 3). The chi-square test gives chi^2 = 12.63, df = 1,
p = 0.0004 (Cramer's V = 0.725), but because of the low expected counts the
exact test is preferred: Fisher exact p = 0.0013 (Yates p = 0.0025), same
conclusion. Section x Passed has minimum expected count 1.67 (3 of 6 cells <
5), chi^2 = 2.02, p = 0.364, so it is unreliable. Goodness-of-fit of the pass
rate to a 70/30 benchmark: observed 19/5 vs expected 16.8/7.2, chi^2 = 0.96,
p = 0.327 (not significantly different; expected counts are adequate). The
70/30 benchmark is an assumed standard, not a fact in the data.

Would the study have enough power to detect a small group difference?
No. Power rises with sample size, effect size and alpha, and falls with
variability. With 16 vs 8 students and alpha = 0.05, power is only 0.07 for
d = 0.2, 0.20 for d = 0.5, 0.42 for d = 0.8 and 0.75 for d = 1.2; the minimum
effect detectable with 80% power is d approx 1.27. About 394 students per
group would be needed for d = 0.2. The observed effect (d = 2.7) is huge, so
detecting it is not in doubt, but a small effect would be missed. Cautious
conclusion: in these 24 synthetic records, students attending >= 75% had much
higher exam scores and pass rates; the evidence is strong for association, but
generalisation, causation and small-effect claims are not supported (small n,
unbalanced groups, one study session, a synthetic dataset).
